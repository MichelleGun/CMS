Onboard Hoop Policy Anatomy

Everything that decides what the drone does when it flies by its own camera: what it sees, how its neural network is built, what comes out, how that becomes a stick command on the real Tello, how it was trained and rewarded, and which trained version actually flew. Every item says where to find it in the code and in ClearML.

Paths are relative to the CMS/ folder. Line numbers checked on 2026-10-12. Numbers like log_std and steps were read from the saved model files.

1. The big picture

The job: fly a small DJI Tello through a 30 cm blue foam ring hanging in a 2 × 2 × 2 m net cage, using only the drone's front camera. The drone is only 14 cm wide, so it has 8 cm of room on each side.

Fifteen times a second the same loop runs:

  1. See. The camera image goes to a ring detector, which finds the blue ellipse and measures where it is, how big it looks and how round it looks.
  2. Describe. Those measurements, plus the drone's own telemetry (height, speed, tilt) and a short memory, are packed into a list of 37 numbers. This is the "observation".
  3. Decide. A small neural network, the actor, turns the 37 numbers into 4 numbers between −1 and +1: slide left/right, forward/back, up/down, turn.
  4. Act. Each number is multiplied by 30 and sent to the Tello as a remote-control stick command.

The network learns in a simulator that copies this loop: a pretend drone, a pretend ring and a pretend camera feed the same 37 numbers. A second network, the critic, only exists during training, where it scores how good each situation is.

The observation builder and the actor are byte-for-byte the same in both worlds. Everything that differs between them (camera, detector, drone physics) is outside the blue boxes. That gap is where sim-to-real problems come from.

2. Inputs: the 37 numbers

The network never sees the camera picture. It sees 37 numbers built from the detector's measurements and the Tello's telemetry. Using numbers instead of pixels makes training fast on a CPU, but it means the policy can only ever know what the detector chose to measure.

Every number is scaled (normalised) so a typical flight keeps it roughly between −1 and +1. Neural networks learn badly when one input is in the hundreds and another is 0.01.

3. The brain: actor and critic

The trainer uses Stable-Baselines3's standard MlpPolicy. "MLP" means a plain stack of fully-connected layers: every number in one layer is connected to every number in the next. There is no memory inside the network (no LSTM); the only memory is the hand-made fields in the 37 inputs.

There are two separate networks that share nothing:

  • The actor decides what to do. It is the only part that flies the drone.
  • The critic (value network) guesses the total future reward from this situation. PPO uses it to judge whether an action turned out better or worse than expected. It is ignored at flight time.
Layer shapes read from policy.pth inside results/hoop/runs/onboard_v5_20261011-225528/best_model.zip: policy_net 64×37, 64×64; action_net 4×64; value_net 64×37, 64×64, 1×64; log_std 4. Totals: actor 6,856 parameters, critic 6,657. A 33-input run has 64×33 in the first layer instead.

5. How it learns: PPO + cloning

Training uses PPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation) with an extra behaviour-cloning pull towards demonstrations. The class is BCRegularizedPPO in hardware/onboard/train_onboard.py:184.

PPO in plain words

  1. Try things. Let the current policy (with its exploration noise) fly 8 simulators for 2048 steps each.
  2. Judge each move. For every step, compare what actually happened (rewards that followed) with what the critic expected. If it went better than expected, that move gets a positive advantage; worse, negative. γ (gamma) = 0.99 says how much future rewards count (a reward 100 steps ≈ 6.7 s ahead counts 37 %). λ (lambda) = 0.95 smooths the estimate (GAE).
  3. Nudge the policy. Make good moves more likely and bad moves less likely, but never change any move's probability by more than ±20 % in one round (the "clip"). That cap is the "proximal" in PPO and stops one lucky batch wrecking the policy.
  4. Train the critic on the same data so its guesses get better.
  5. Repeat.

Behaviour cloning (the clone that starts a run)

6. What it is rewarded for

Reward is the only thing that tells PPO what "good" means. Most of it is shaping: small payments every tick for getting closer to the right path. "Potential-based" means only progress is paid. Circling or hovering earns nothing, because going away loses what coming back earns.

9. Why it flew away: checklist

What the logs and the code say about "it saw the ring, got close, then flew away". These are ranked by how much evidence there is, not proven causes. Each one points to what to check or change.

1. The versions you flew are the ones built before this exact bug was fixed. All three policies flown on 2026-10-12 are 33-input runs. The pushed and heading memory (34 and 37 inputs) were added because the policy "lined up but would not commit" and "stopped just past the plane". One of them, …-024236, had only 30,000 steps of training (its noise level never moved from the start value).
Check: tello_*/meta.json "model". But: the newer 37-input runs score worse in simulation (section 8), so flying them is not a fix on its own.

2. Close up, the real detector stops looking like the simulator's. In flight tello_20261012-184521 the drone closed from 2.9 m to 0.38 m, then at t ≈ 23.6 s the ring's roundness dropped from 0.9 to 0.47 and its centre jumped to the right edge (cx 0.77). The policy answered with a hard sideways slide (lr −0.78, stick −23) and backed away. In the simulator, close-range detections are clean geometry; on the real drone the ring is overflowing the frame and the ellipse fit is unreliable.
Check: ClearML Tello/flights → ring/axis_ratio, ring/cx, policy_action/lr around the end of each flight. Fix ideas: train with the real detector in the loop (--render), add close-range detector noise / clipping to sim.py, or record real close approaches as demos.

3. Exploration has collapsed. Every long run has log_std ≈ −2.3 to −2.8, so the random noise is only about 6–10 % of full stick. With ent_coef = 0 nothing pushes back. The policy can no longer discover new behaviour, which matches the newer runs getting worse (0.65 → 0.22 → 0.06 passes) while training for tens of millions more steps.
Check: ClearML Scalars → train/std and train/entropy_loss on new runs. Fix ideas: ent_coef 0.001–0.01, or reset log_std to about −1 when continuing a run.

4. Ranges outside training. The real detector reported the ring at 3–3.7 m in a 2 m cage on some flights. Range is capped at 2 m in the input, so those read as "far / not found", a situation the policy only ever saw while searching. You chose to keep the current detector settings for now; this stays on the list.

Related: the saved hardware/onboard/detector.json has min_major_px = 200. A 42 cm ring is 200 px wide only when it is closer than about 1.44 m, so the drone cannot see the ring from further away than that. The simulator was trained seeing it from the whole cage. With these settings four dry-run tests also stop detecting the ring (test_fly_hoop / test_human); they pass with the default detector.

5. Possible sign or unit mismatches between sim and Tello (not yet checked). The simulator's yaw is counter-clockwise-positive (sim.py:532, :572); if the real Tello reports yaw clockwise-positive, the yaw-rate input and the pass-heading input have the opposite sign on the real drone. Same question for vgy, roll, pitch, and whether vgx is cm/s or dm/s (obs.py:25).
Check, on the ground: turn the drone clockwise by hand and watch yaw in telemetry.csv; push it forward and watch vgx.

6. The task got harder faster than the network. v5 asks for same-side laps from 9 start zones with full randomisation, using the same 2 × 64 network with no internal memory.
Fix ideas: go back to front-runway, single-pass training (--laps 1 --start-mode ring) until passes are reliable, then add difficulty; try net_arch=[128,128].

7. Unmeasured physics. 125 rc per m/s, the 70° field of view and the response lag are all assumptions (section 7). Domain randomisation covers ±25 % speed, not more.

8. Noisy model selection. best_model.zip is picked from 18 evaluation episodes. One or two lucky passes can decide which checkpoint is "best".

Tier 2: structural, if Tier 1 is not enough

Aliasing (§3.1)

Front and back of the ring look the same, the lecture gridworld exactly. The memory fields (section 2) are a hand-made agent state. Options: frame stacking (Atari notebook) or a recurrent policy. The notebook tests whether noise beats the deterministic mean; if it does, aliasing is real.

Cloning pull is off-policy (§7.9)

Curriculum (§8.1)

If the policy cannot pass, its own data teaches nothing. Raise difficulty (laps, zones, dr) only when the pass rate clears a threshold.

Reward scale

16. The ring tracker (a belief state)

The problem every method hit. A pass works, the loop does not: RL laps stage 0.02 laps per episode, DAgger 0–0.11, human runs without loops. On the way round the outside the ring is out of view, and the 37 inputs then look the same wherever the ring really is. The task is a POMDP (partially observable): the policy's input is not the state. The standard answer is to give the agent a belief state, a best guess of what it cannot see.

Critic V(s)Ring tracker
Answers"How good is this situation?""Where is the ring right now?"
Learned?Yes, by RLNo: geometry
UsedOnly in training, to judge actionsEvery tick, in training and on the real drone

How it works (dead reckoning). At each fresh detection the ring's position in the drone's frame comes straight from the camera: forward = the range (from the ring's apparent size), right = cx·tan(35°)·range, up = −cy·tan(35°)·range. While the ring is out of view, the estimate is moved by what the drone did: turned (telemetry yaw, which the real Tello reports reliably), climbed (height), and flew forward/sideways (the executed commands × the nominal speed, the same odometer as pushed). The policy gets 4 extra inputs, ring forward/right/up and "seen yet", so 41 in all. It is opt-in (--ring-tracker): appended after the 37, so a 37-input policy reads the first 37 unchanged and an old model is widened with zero weights (it starts out flying exactly as before).

Link to the Week 10 material. The PPO Atari notebook stacks 4 frames because one frame does not show which way the ball moves: the same partial-observability problem, solved with a short memory. Frame stacking is too short here (the ring is out of view for seconds); a recurrent policy (LSTM) would learn its own memory but needs far more data. The tracker writes the memory from known geometry. Textbook: Sutton & Barto §17.3.

Round 5: the teacher shows the way round, and finding the ring pays

  1. Teacher labels on the loop leg (DAgger-style relabelling, Ross et al. 2011):
    • our policy (P) flies; every tick after its first pass, the privileged teacher records what it would do there;
    • near the noodle or the net, the teacher takes the sticks for 1 s;
    • 60 episodes gave 56,625 labelled ticks (the teacher flew 5 % of them);
    • these labels feed a small, decaying imitation term inside PPO: coef 0.2 → 0.02 over 2M steps. The RL reward stays in charge.

Why this is not the DAgger that failed before (§14, §16): there the student had to copy the teacher's precise final push through an 8 cm gap from imitation alone, and couldn't. Here the push is RL's, which it does well (~2/3 of episodes). The teacher only teaches the coarse part, going round and turning back, and only as a hint that fades out. Pre-pass ticks are not labelled at all.

23. Optimising the loop policy (the optimiser)

From 2026-10-17 a script, loop_forever.py, keeps improving the policy by itself. Each round it takes the best policy so far (the parent), trains two copies of it for 2M more steps (the parent's own recipe, and one new idea), scores both, and keeps whichever is best. This is the same idea as a race (§15), repeated: successive improvement with a fixed referee.

Why the stress drone helped. A slower, driftier drone punishes a late, slanted entry: by the time the command acts the drone has drifted onto the noodle. Training against it pushes the policy to commit more decisively and to recover faster, and that also helped loops in the normal sim. Why loop practice helped now but not in §20: with memory the policy can use the practice: it knows where it is on the way round.

Three things we learned about the referee.

  • Same seed, same run. Continuing the same model with the same seed trains the identical policy. Rounds 4 and 5 both gave exactly 2.155, so one round was wasted. Each round now uses its own seed.
  • Noise. One 36-episode evaluation wobbles by about ±0.15 laps/ep. A "new best" by less than that can be luck. Now every candidate, and the parent, is re-scored on the same 72 episodes plus 60 slanted starts, and a challenger must win by more than 0.10.
  • What you reward is what you get. The score counted loops fully and corners at half weight. Loops went 1.58 → 2.33 while clean corner entries fell 67 % → 57 %, the opposite of what the real drone needs. Since 2026-10-23 the score is laps/ep + 1.0 × oblique entry.

Round 2 of the optimiser (from 2026-10-23) builds on C9 with ideas aimed at the corners: CZ (reward lining up on the axis and keeping clear of the noodle), CO (even more slanted starts), CE (C9 + stress drone); at loops: CD (DAgger relabel of C9's own states); and at stability: CL (a gentler learning rate). It restarts by itself at logon and never runs twice. The running results are in HANDOVER.md §7.

24. Hierarchical RL: skills and a manager

The loop task has phases: find the hoop, line up and go through, then fly round the outside and find it again. One flat policy has to do all of them with the same network, and training showed a trade-off. Practising the loop made loops better but slanted (corner) entries worse (67 % → 57 %). A reward for lining up helped corners but made loops worse. Hierarchical RL splits the problem: each phase gets its own skill, and a manager learns which skill to use when.

We use the options framework (Sutton, Precup & Singh, 1999):

  • An option is a skill run for a while: here a memory policy that flies for up to 8 ticks (0.53 s), or until a pass.
  • The manager is a policy over options. Its world is a semi-MDP: one step = one whole option, and its reward = the sum of the rewards while that option flew. Since one decision covers 8 ticks, its discount is 0.92 per decision, about 0.99 per tick.
  • The skills are frozen while the manager learns. That is what makes the manager's job small: 3 choices every half second instead of 4 continuous stick values 15 times a second.

A fair test. The learned manager has to beat two things on the same referee as the optimiser (72 laps episodes + 60 slanted starts, score = laps/ep + corner entry):

  • the flat policy C9;
  • a fixed switch: the enter skill until the first pass, then the loop skill. The switch reads "a pass happened" from the drone's own pass detector (telemetry), so it works on the real drone too.

If the fixed switch already wins, the gain comes from the skills. If the learned manager wins on top of that, it has learned something about when to switch (for example, back to "enter" when a loop goes wrong).
